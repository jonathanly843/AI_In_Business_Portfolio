About This Portfolio

A Quarter in AI

This portfolio documents seven hands-on labs I completed in MIS 432: AI in Business at Western Washington University during Spring 2026. Each lab challenged me to go beyond surface-level AI hype and examine how a real company — from a drug-discovery startup to a self-driving car fleet — actually deploys machine learning, algorithmic decision-making, and agentic AI in production environments. I built interactive case-study websites for companies including Netflix, Spotify, Uber, Waymo, Airbnb, and Epic Healthcare, researching the data pipelines, model architectures, and governance frameworks that underpin each system. What I took away most was the persistent gap between what AI promises in a press release and what it takes to ship a system that is accurate, fair, and operationally reliable at scale.

Quarter Reflections

Key Takeaways

  • 01 AI is only as good as its data pipeline. Every company in this portfolio invested as heavily in data infrastructure — labeling, cleaning, feature engineering, and real-time ingestion — as in the models themselves. A brilliant architecture trained on bad or incomplete data consistently underperforms a simpler model trained on high-quality, representative data.
  • 02 Every AI system needs a human-in-the-loop governance plan. From Waymo's safety drivers to Epic's clinical oversight requirements, every production AI system I studied had explicit mechanisms for human review, override, and accountability. Governance is not a compliance checkbox — it's load-bearing architecture.
  • 03 Business value comes from workflow integration, not model accuracy alone. Spotify's recommendations and Airbnb's pricing tools create value because they are deeply embedded in the product experience — not because they achieve a high benchmark score in isolation. The last mile of AI deployment, connecting a model's output to a real decision a user takes action on, is often harder than training the model.
  • 04 The gap between AI hype and implementation reality is measurable in cost, time, and risk. Lab after lab, I found that the companies doing AI well had accepted long timelines, expensive data infrastructure, and the possibility of failure. The ones in the news for AI problems had often skipped these steps. Credible AI strategy means being honest about all three.
